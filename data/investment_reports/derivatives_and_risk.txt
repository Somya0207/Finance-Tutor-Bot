Derivatives and Risk Management

A derivative is a financial contract whose value depends on an underlying asset such as a stock, bond, commodity, currency or index.

A forward contract is a private agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date. A futures contract is a standardised version traded on an exchange and settled daily through margin accounts, which reduces counterparty risk.

An option gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a strike price on or before an expiry date. A call option gives the right to buy; a put option gives the right to sell. The price paid for the option is called the premium.

The maximum loss for the buyer of an option is the premium paid. The seller (writer) of an uncovered call option faces theoretically unlimited loss.

Option prices depend on the underlying price, the strike price, time to expiry, volatility, and interest rates. The Black-Scholes model is a widely used formula for pricing European options.

A swap is an agreement to exchange cash flows. In an interest rate swap, one party pays a fixed rate and receives a floating rate, which lets firms convert floating-rate debt into fixed-rate debt.

Hedging means taking a position that offsets an existing risk, for example an airline buying fuel futures to lock in costs. Speculation means taking on risk in the hope of profit.

Value at Risk (VaR) estimates the maximum loss over a given time horizon at a chosen confidence level. For example, a one-day 95% VaR of 2 million means the portfolio is expected to lose more than 2 million on only about 5% of days.
